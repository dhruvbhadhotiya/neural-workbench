GAN basics: a game between two networks

— · Lesson 1 of 4 · Exact math labs

A classifier and a forger improve together. By the end of this lesson you can write both losses, explain the equilibrium, and watch a real 1-D GAN train from random weights.

Objectives

  • Write the minimax objective and the non-saturating generator objective, with every symbol defined.
  • Compute the optimal discriminator \(D^\ast(x)\) from two densities and plug in numbers.
  • Explain why both losses settle near \(\log 2\) rather than at zero.
  • Train a 1-D GAN in the browser and read its histogram, decision curve and loss curves.

You know this from ML → here it becomes

In ML you fit \(p(y\mid x)\): a label is given and a model predicts it. A GAN has no labels. It learns to sample from \(p_{\text{data}}(x)\) by training a generator \(G\) to fool a discriminator \(D\). Binary cross-entropy is still the loss — what is new is that the targets come from a competitor whose weights change at the same time.

Intuition: the forger and the detective

The generator \(G\) maps noise \(\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) to a sample \(G(\mathbf{z})\). The noise dimension is \(D_z=100\) for image GANs; in the lab below we use \(D_z=1\) so we can draw the whole distribution. The discriminator \(D\) receives either a real sample \(\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}\) or a fake sample \(G(\mathbf{z})\) and outputs \(D(\cdot)\in(0,1)\), its probability that the input is real.

\(D\) is trained by ordinary binary cross-entropy with labels 1 (real) and 0 (fake). \(G\) is trained on the same network \(D\), but with the opposite goal: make \(D(G(\mathbf{z}))\) close to 1. When \(G\) improves, \(D\) sees harder fakes; when \(D\) improves, \(G\) gets a sharper signal. At equilibrium neither can improve alone.

Press Run and watch three panels. Left: the target density is drawn as a smooth curve and the generated samples as a histogram of the same scale. Middle: the discriminator's decision curve \(D(x)\) and the theoretical optimum \(D^\ast(x)=p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}}+p_g)\); the generated density \(p_g\) is estimated from the histogram with a small kernel. Right: both losses, lightly smoothed so the trend is visible. The two losses oscillate around \(\log 2\approx 0.693\), not zero — that is what a balanced game looks like.

At equilibrium the generator's samples match the data distribution, so the best any discriminator can do is guess: \(D^\ast(x)=\tfrac12\) everywhere and the value is \(-\log 4\).

The math

1 · The minimax game

\[ \min_G\max_D V(D,G)= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(\mathbf{x})]}_{\text{real samples}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_{\mathbf z}}\bigl[\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))\bigr]}_{\text{fake samples}} \]

Here \(p_{\text{data}}\) is the unknown data distribution, \(p_{\mathbf z}\) the fixed noise distribution, \(G\) the generator with parameters \(\theta_G\), and \(D\) the discriminator with parameters \(\theta_D\). The expectation \(\mathbb{E}\) is an average over samples. \(\log\) is the natural logarithm; the loss is reported as a mean over the batch of \(N\) samples. Maximising \(V\) over \(D\) means making \(\log D(\mathbf{x})\) large on real data and \(\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))\) large on fakes.

2 · The optimal discriminator

\[ D^\ast(\mathbf{x})=\frac{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})} {p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})+p_g(\mathbf{x})} \]

\(p_g\) is the distribution of the generated samples. To see where this comes from, fix \(G\) and write the integrand for one location \(x\): with \(a=p_{\text{data}}(x)\) and \(b=p_g(x)\), the contribution to \(V\) is \(a\log d+b\log(1-d)\) where \(d=D(x)\). Differentiating in \(d\) and setting to zero gives \(a/d-b/(1-d)=0\), hence \(d=a/(a+b)\).

Worked example. Take three locations with these densities:

Optimal discriminator from two densities
\(x\)\(p_{\text{data}}(x)=a\)\(p_g(x)=b\)\(D^\ast(x)=a/(a+b)\)
\(-1\)0.600.15\(0.60/0.75=0.800\)
\(0\)0.100.30\(0.10/0.40=0.250\)
\(1\)0.020.20\(0.02/0.22=0.0909\)

Where data is common and fakes are rare, \(D^\ast\) is near 1; where fakes dominate, it is near 0. The lab draws exactly this curve from the current histogram.

3 · Discriminator loss on three samples

\[ \mathcal{L}_D=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \bigl[y_n\log d_n+(1-y_n)\log(1-d_n)\bigr],\qquad d_n=D(x_n) \]

This is binary cross-entropy (BCE) with labels \(y_n\in\{0,1\}\) and mean reduction. In code the network returns a logit \(z_n\) and we use the fused BCE-with-logits loss, which is algebraically the same but numerically stable.

Worked example. \(N=3\): two real samples with \(d_1=0.9\), \(d_2=0.2\), one fake sample with \(d_3=0.3\) (so \(y=(1,1,0)\)). Then

\[ \mathcal{L}_D=-\frac{\log 0.9+\log 0.2+\log(1-0.3)}{3} =-\frac{-0.10536-1.60944-0.35667}{3} =\frac{2.07147}{3}=0.69049 \]

Notice that the badly classified real sample \(d_2=0.2\) contributes 1.60944 of the total 2.07147: BCE punishes confident mistakes hard.

4 · The generator loss people actually use

\[ \text{minimax:}\quad \mathcal{L}_G^{\text{sat}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf z}\bigl[\log(1-D(G(\mathbf z)))\bigr], \qquad \text{non-saturating:}\quad \mathcal{L}_G^{\text{ns}}=-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf z}\bigl[\log D(G(\mathbf z))\bigr] \]

Both push \(D(G(\mathbf z))\) up, but the gradients behave differently. If the discriminator is confidently right and \(D(G(\mathbf z))=0.3\), then \(\log(1-0.3)=\log 0.7=-0.35667\) is already almost flat, while \(-\log 0.3=1.20397\) still has a useful slope. That is why the non-saturating form is the default in practice; it is the one used in the lab and in the code listing.

At equilibrium \(p_g=p_{\text{data}}\), so \(D^\ast=\tfrac12\) and \(\mathcal{L}_G^{\text{ns}}=-\log\tfrac12=\log 2=0.69315\). The lab's two loss curves should therefore hover near the same height — that is a feature, not a bug.

5 · What the value of the game measures

Substitute \(D^\ast\) back into \(V\). After two lines of algebra (omitted here) the optimum value is \(V(D^\ast,G)=-\log 4+2\,D_{\text{JS}}(p_{\text{data}}\Vert p_g)\), where \(D_{\text{JS}}(p\Vert q)=\tfrac12 D_{\text{KL}}(p\Vert m)+\tfrac12 D_{\text{KL}}(q\Vert m)\) with \(m=(p+q)/2\) and \(D_{\text{KL}}(p\Vert q)=\sum_i p_i\log(p_i/q_i)\). Minimising the game therefore minimises Jensen–Shannon divergence, which is zero exactly when the two distributions match.

Worked example. Let \(p=(0.5,0.5)\), \(q=(0.25,0.75)\). Then \(m=(0.375,0.625)\).

\[ D_{\text{KL}}(p\Vert m)=0.5\log\tfrac{0.5}{0.375}+0.5\log\tfrac{0.5}{0.625}=0.03227 \] \[ D_{\text{KL}}(q\Vert m)=0.25\log\tfrac{0.25}{0.375}+0.75\log\tfrac{0.75}{0.625}=0.03537 \] \[ D_{\text{JS}}=\tfrac12(0.03227+0.03537)=0.03382,\qquad V(D^\ast,G)=-\log 4+2(0.03382)=-1.31865 \]

Symbols used in this lesson

\(N\) batch size · \(G\), \(D\) generator and discriminator · \(\theta_G,\theta_D\) their parameters · \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_z}\) latent noise · \(D_z\) latent dimension · \(p_{\mathbf z}=\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) noise distribution · \(p_{\text{data}}\) data distribution · \(p_g\) generated distribution · \(d\in(0,1)\) discriminator probability · \(y\in\{0,1\}\) real/fake label · \(\mathcal{L}\) mean loss · \(\eta\) learning rate · \(D_{\text{KL}}\), \(D_{\text{JS}}\) divergences.

Code

A complete 1-D GAN in PyTorch. The target is a two-mode mixture generated in the script — no download. The crucial line is fake.detach() in the discriminator update: it turns the generator's output into a plain tensor so \(D\)'s step does not change \(G\)'s weights. Both networks output logits and use the fused BCE loss. 

import torch
from torch import nn

torch.manual_seed(0)

# Synthetic 1-D data: two Gaussians at -1.2 and +1.2.  Shape (512, 1).
x_real = (torch.randn(512, 1) * 0.3
          + torch.where(torch.rand(512, 1) > 0.5,
                        torch.tensor(1.2), torch.tensor(-1.2)))

def block(i, o):
    return nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(i, o), nn.Tanh())

G = nn.Sequential(block(1, 16), block(16, 16), nn.Linear(16, 1))
D = nn.Sequential(block(1, 16), block(16, 16), nn.Linear(16, 1))
opt_g = torch.optim.Adam(G.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
opt_d = torch.optim.Adam(D.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
bce = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()          # logits in, mean over the batch

for step in range(2000):
    z = torch.randn(32, 1)            # (N, D_z) = (32, 1)
    fake = G(z)                       # (N, 1): G's graph is alive

    # --- discriminator step: real -> 1, fake -> 0 -----------------------
    opt_d.zero_grad()
    real_idx = torch.randint(0, len(x_real), (32,))
    d_real = D(x_real[real_idx])      # (32, 1) logits
    d_fake = D(fake.detach())         # detach: do NOT train G here
    loss_d = bce(d_real, torch.ones_like(d_real)) \
           + bce(d_fake, torch.zeros_like(d_fake))
    loss_d.backward()
    opt_d.step()

    # --- generator step: make D(G(z)) -> 1 ------------------------------
    opt_g.zero_grad()
    d_on_fake = D(G(z))               # fresh sample; D's grads are harmless
    loss_g = bce(d_on_fake, torch.ones_like(d_on_fake))
    loss_g.backward()                 # backprop through D into G
    opt_g.step()

if step % 100 == 0:
    with torch.no_grad():
        samples = G(torch.randn(4096, 1)).squeeze(1)
    print(step, round(loss_d.item(), 4), round(loss_g.item(), 4),
          round(samples.mean().item(), 3), round(samples.std().item(), 3))

print("G params", sum(p.numel() for p in G.parameters()))   # 2-layer MLP, 1 -> 16 -> 16 -> 1
print("D params", sum(p.numel() for p in D.parameters()))

The two losses should settle near \(\log 2 \approx 0.693\), not zero. Different seeds give different sample histograms; this toy problem is for understanding the game, not for measuring quality.

Common mistakes

Training \(D\) to convergence each step

A perfect \(D\) gives almost zero gradient to \(G\). Take one \(D\) step (or a few) per \(G\) step, not hundreds. A discriminator accuracy near 100% early on means \(D\) is winning too hard.

BCE on logits

Applying sigmoid and then BCELoss computes the log of a saturated probability. Output logits and use BCEWithLogitsLoss; the browser lab uses the fused version in DL.nn.MLP too.

Forgetting detach()

In the \(D\) update, D(fake) backpropagates into \(G\) unless you detach. You then optimise the wrong gradient — and both losses can fall together for a while, which looks like success but is not the game you meant to play.

Same learning rate by reflex

\(G\) and \(D\) have different tasks and can learn at different speeds. The two-timescale rule (TTUR) deliberately uses a different \(\eta\) for each. Start with both near \(10^{-3}\) here, then tune them separately.

Debug this

A reader reports: “both my generator and discriminator losses fall smoothly toward zero, and my fake samples are all the same number.” The code is the listing above with one line changed.

Show the likely cause and the fix

The fake.detach() call was removed in the discriminator update. Now the discriminator's gradients flow into the generator, so the same gradient step is trying to make \(D\) both accept and reject the fakes: the two networks collude on a constant output that the discriminator can classify perfectly, and both losses collapse together. Restore fake.detach() in the \(D\) update, and keep the \(G\) update on a freshly generated G(z) so its graph is intact.

Second thing to check: a generator whose output is unbounded can run far outside the data range. In the lab the generator ends in \(\tanh\) scaled to the data span, which keeps the game on the same canvas.

Summary

1 · The GAN trains G to sample p_data by maximising a binary classification objective that D simultaneously minimises.
2 · The optimal discriminator is p_data/(p_data+p_g); at equilibrium it is 1/2 and both BCE losses sit near log 2.
3 · Use BCE with logits, detach fake samples in D's step, and the non-saturating loss for G.

Cheat sheet — M3·01

  • \(\min_G\max_D V=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\log D(x)+\mathbb{E}_{z}\log(1-D(G(z)))\).
  • \(D^\ast(x)=p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}}+p_g)\); if \(p_g=p_{\text{data}}\) then \(D^\ast=\tfrac12\).
  • Non-saturating: \(\mathcal{L}_G=-\mathbb{E}_z\log D(G(z))\); at equilibrium \(=\log 2\approx0.69315\).
  • \(V(D^\ast,G)=-\log4+2D_{\text{JS}}\); JS = 0 iff the distributions match.
  • Two updates per step: D on real(1)+fake(0) with fake.detach(), then G on fresh G(z) labelled 1.
  • Logits in, BCEWithLogitsLoss out — never sigmoid twice.
