Training and evaluating GANs

— · Lesson 2 of 4 · Exact math labs

Loss curves lie. This lesson gives you the diagnostics that tell a healthy game from a collapsed one, and the distribution-level metrics that judge the samples rather than the loss.

Objectives

  • Recognise mode collapse and an unbalanced loss tug-of-war from readouts and plots.
  • Compute a 2-D Fréchet distance from means and covariances, and state what image FID changes.
  • Explain what IS measures, why it can be fooled, and how precision and recall cover different failures.
  • Choose diagnostics (D accuracy, gradient norms, EMA, TTUR) that actually guide a fix.

You know this from ML → here it becomes

In supervised ML, a held-out accuracy number tells you if training worked. For a GAN there are no labels, and the generator's loss can improve while its samples get worse. Evaluation moves from “is this prediction right?” to “does this cloud of samples match that cloud?”

Intuition: what goes wrong, and what to look at

A GAN has two failure modes that look nothing like a supervised training curve. In mode collapse, the generator finds a few outputs that reliably fool \(D\) and puts all its probability mass there: samples look sharp but cover almost none of the data. In an unbalanced game, one network wins: \(D\) at 100% accuracy gives \(G\) no gradient, while a weak \(D\) gives \(G\) a useless signal.

The next two labs are illustrative: they are schematic traces that show the shape of these failures, not trained networks. The third lab is exact: it computes a Fréchet distance from the numbers you set.

Scrub the slider from step 0 to 100. The target is a ring of eight equally likely Gaussian modes. As the trace advances, weight drains from most modes into one or two — the classic signature. A single beautiful sample grid cannot reveal this; you need many samples and a coverage statistic.

Switch regimes and compare. A healthy game oscillates around the same level for both players. When \(D\) wins, its loss falls toward zero and \(G\)'s grows without bound. When \(G\) wins, the mirror image happens. Neither extreme means “better training”; both mean the game has stopped being informative.

The math

1 · Inception Score

\[ \mathrm{IS}=\exp\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_g} \bigl[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(p(y\mid\mathbf{x})\Vert \bar p(y)\bigr)\bigr]\Bigr), \qquad \bar p(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_g}p(y\mid\mathbf{x}) \]

A pretrained image classifier supplies \(p(y\mid\mathbf{x})\), the class distribution of one generated image: a sharp prediction means the image looks like a clear object. The marginal \(\bar p(y)\) averages those predictions over the whole generated set: a diverse set has a spread-out \(\bar p(y)\). Large KL between the two means “sharp images with varied classes”. \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)=\sum_i p_i\log(p_i/q_i)\); entropy \(H(p)=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i\) makes the worked example short.

Worked example. An ImageNet-style classifier gives three samples \(p_1=(0.1,0.9)\), \(p_2=(0.8,0.2)\), \(p_3=(0.4,0.6)\).

\[ H(p_1)=0.32508,\quad H(p_2)=0.50040,\quad H(p_3)=0.67301 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbb{E}H(p_i)=0.49950 \] \[ \bar p=\tfrac13(p_1+p_2+p_3)=(0.4333,0.5667),\qquad H(\bar p)=0.68423 \] \[ \mathbb{E}D_{\mathrm{KL}}=H(\bar p)-\mathbb{E}H(p_i)=0.68423-0.49950=0.18473, \qquad \mathrm{IS}=e^{0.18473}=1.20290 \]

Why is IS flawed? It uses a fixed classifier (usually ImageNet-trained), so it rewards images that look like its classes rather than images like the target data; it ignores real data entirely; and it collapses to a low value if every generated image gets the same label, even when the images are otherwise diverse. It also needs many samples for a stable marginal.

2 · Fréchet Inception Distance

\[ \mathrm{FID}=\lVert\mu_r-\mu_g\rVert^2+ \mathrm{Tr}\bigl(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\bigr) \]

\(\mu_r,\Sigma_r\) are the mean and covariance of real images in a feature space, and \(\mu_g,\Sigma_g\) those of generated images in the same space. For image FID the feature space is the penultimate activations of an Inception network; lower is better, and identical Gaussian clouds give FID = 0. The formula is stated, not derived; the lab computes it exactly for 2-D clouds you control.

Worked example (2-D, diagonal covariances). Let \(\mu_r=(1,2)\), \(\mu_g=(1.5,2.5)\), \(\Sigma_r=I_2\), \(\Sigma_g=\mathrm{diag}(4,1)\). Then

\[ \lVert\mu_r-\mu_g\rVert^2=(1.5-1)^2+(2.5-2)^2=0.25+0.25=0.50 \] \[ (\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}=\mathrm{diag}(2,1) \] \[ \mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}) =(1+4-2\cdot2)+(1+1-2\cdot1)=1+0=1 \] \[ \mathrm{FID}=0.50+1=1.50 \]

FID depends on the feature extractor, the image preprocessing, the sample count and the crop/resize pipeline. Never compare two numbers computed with different settings.

3 · Precision and recall for distributions

Borrow the words from classification but apply them to modes. Precision asks: of the generated samples, how many look like something in the data? Recall asks: of the data modes, how many are represented by generated samples? These are different failure axes. A generator producing one perfect-looking mode has high precision and low recall; one spraying samples over the whole space has high recall and low precision.

Worked example. Suppose the data has 8 modes and your generator covers 5 of them: recall \(=5/8=0.625\). Of 10 sampled clusters, 7 fall within the tolerance of a real mode: precision \(=7/10=0.70\). A single FID number cannot tell you which of the two errors occurred.

4 · Diagnostics that guide a fix

Track discriminator accuracy on a balanced real/fake batch: it should hover near 50%, not race to 100% by step 10. Track gradient norms of both networks: a norm collapsing to zero means that player has stopped learning. Two common remedies are TTUR (the two-timescale update rule), which uses a larger \(\eta_D\) than \(\eta_G\), and an exponential moving average (EMA) of the generator weights for evaluation, which smooths the samples without changing the game. None of these replace looking at many samples from several checkpoints.

Symbols used in this lesson

\(p_g\) generated distribution · \(p(y\mid\mathbf{x})\) classifier's class distribution for a generated image · \(\bar p(y)\) average prediction over generated images · \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\) Kullback–Leibler divergence · \(H\) entropy in nats · \(\mu_r,\mu_g\) real and generated feature means · \(\Sigma_r,\Sigma_g\) feature covariances · \(\mathrm{Tr}\) matrix trace · \(N\) number of samples · \(\eta_D,\eta_G\) discriminator and generator learning rates.

Code

A diagnostic loop that logs the numbers discussed above, plus an offline, dependency-free FID calculation on 2-D features. The torchmetrics line is shown as architecture only: it fetches an Inception model when first used, so it is not part of this offline lesson. 

import math
import torch

def fid_2d(real, fake):
    """FID on a (N, 2) feature batch. No downloads; formula from the lesson."""
    mr, mf = real.mean(0), fake.mean(0)
    sr = torch.cov(real.T)
    sf = torch.cov(fake.T)
    delta = float(((mr - mf) ** 2).sum())
    # sqrtm needs a complex view for numerical stability in older torch builds
    root, _ = torch.linalg.sqrtm((sr @ sf).to(torch.complex64))
    trace = float(torch.trace(sr + sf - 2 * root.real))
    return delta + trace

def diagnostics(G, D, z, x_real, step):
    with torch.no_grad():
        fake = G(z)                          # (N, 1) in the toy setting
        acc_real = (D(x_real) > 0).float().mean()
        acc_fake = (D(fake) <= 0).float().mean()
        d_acc = 0.5 * float(acc_real + acc_fake)
        sample_std = float(fake.std())
    G.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)            # gradients accumulate in PyTorch
    g = D(fake).mean()
    g.backward()                             # inspect G's gradient norm after D's backward
    last_weight = G[-1].weight               # final Linear in nn.Sequential
    gnorm = last_weight.grad.norm().item() if last_weight.grad is not None else 0.0
    if step % 100 == 0:
        print(step, "D acc", round(d_acc, 3), "grad", round(gnorm, 5),
              "sample std", round(sample_std, 3))
    return d_acc, gnorm

# Worked check of the formula with the numbers from the lesson:
r = torch.tensor([[1., 2.]]) + torch.randn(20000, 2)
f = torch.tensor([[1.5, 2.5]]) + torch.randn(20000, 2) * torch.tensor([2., 1.])
print("fid_2d ~", round(fid_2d(r, f), 3))     # near 1.50, sampling noise aside

The printed FID is a sample estimate: with finite batches it fluctuates around the population value 1.50. Reporting FID with too few samples produces a biased, optimistic number.

Common mistakes

Comparing FID across preprocessing

FID from differently cropped, resized or normalised images is not the same metric. Compare only numbers produced by one feature extractor and one pipeline.

Too few samples

A covariance estimated from a small batch is noisy and biased. Use tens of thousands of samples when you can, and report the count with the number.

IS with the wrong classifier

IS with a non-ImageNet model measures that model's favourite classes, not quality on your data. For medical or satellite images the Inception class prior is meaningless.

Judging by one sample grid

A pretty grid can be one lucky mode. Look at many samples across checkpoints, plus a coverage statistic, before declaring collapse fixed.

Debug this

A reader reports: “my discriminator accuracy hits 100% at step 10 and stays there, and the generator loss keeps climbing.” What should they check first?

Show the likely cause and the fix

The discriminator is far too strong for the generator at this point. With perfect separation, \(D(G(\mathbf z))\approx 0\) and the non-saturating gradient is nearly zero: there is nothing left to learn from. Slow \(D\) down (smaller \(\eta_D\), or fewer \(D\) steps per \(G\) step), consider TTUR with \(\eta_D\) and \(\eta_G\) tuned separately, add label smoothing or instance noise, and watch the accuracy return toward 50%.

Also check that real and fake batches have the same preprocessing: a trivially separable scaling or colour difference lets \(D\) win without learning anything about content.

Check yourself

Summary

1 · Loss curves alone cannot tell a healthy GAN from a collapsed or one-sided game; use many samples, D accuracy and coverage.
2 · FID compares the means and covariances of real and fake features; IS rewards sharp, diverse classifier predictions but ignores your data.
3 · Precision measures how real the samples look, recall measures how much of the data is covered — report both with a fixed pipeline.

Cheat sheet — M3·02

  • \(\mathrm{IS}=\exp(\mathbb{E}_x D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(y\mid x)\Vert\bar p(y)))\).
  • \(\mathrm{FID}=\lVert\mu_r-\mu_g\rVert^2+\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2})\).
  • 2-D worked value: means (1,2) vs (1.5,2.5), covariances \(I\) and diag(4,1) → FID = 1.50.
  • Precision = fake samples near real modes; recall = real modes covered by fakes.
  • D accuracy near 100% early: slow D down, try TTUR (\(\eta_D > \eta_G\)) and label smoothing.
  • Keep the feature extractor, preprocessing and sample count fixed when comparing FID.
