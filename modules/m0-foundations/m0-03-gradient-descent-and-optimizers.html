Gradient descent and optimizers

— · Lesson 3 of 5 · Exact math labs

The gradient tells you which way is downhill. The optimizer decides how far to walk, how much momentum to carry, and whether to rescale each direction.

Objectives

  • Distinguish batch gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent and mini-batches.
  • Implement momentum, RMSProp, Adam and AdamW updates by hand on a scalar.
  • Explain bias correction and why AdamW decouples weight decay.
  • Choose a learning rate from the curvature of the loss surface.

You know this from ML → here it becomes

LogisticRegression(solver="lbfgs") hides the update rule because classical ML usually has a well-behaved convex objective. Deep nets are high-dimensional and non-convex, so you pick the rule yourself: plain SGD, momentum, or an adaptive method such as Adam. The gradient is the same object; only the step changes.

Intuition: five ways to walk downhill

Imagine standing on a foggy landscape. The gradient points uphill; subtracting it moves you downhill. Plain SGD takes a fixed stride η along that direction. Momentum is a heavy ball: it keeps rolling in the direction it has been travelling, which smooths noise and accelerates consistent slopes. RMSProp rescales each coordinate by its recent gradient magnitude, so steep directions take smaller effective steps and flat directions take larger ones. Adam combines both ideas. AdamW additionally shrinks the parameters themselves, separately from the gradient.

No optimizer is universally best. On a round bowl SGD is perfectly adequate; in a narrow ravine adaptive methods shine; near a saddle all methods must escape along the negative curvature direction, and momentum can overshoot. The lab below races them from an identical starting point on all three surfaces.

Every optimizer changes only the update, never the loss or the gradient. If the loss is NaN, suspect the learning rate before suspecting the optimizer.

The math

Symbols

\(\theta\) a parameter; \(\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}\) its gradient; \(N\) batch size; \(\eta\) learning rate; \(\mathcal{L}\) loss; \(v\) velocity (momentum buffer); \(m,s\) Adam's first and second moment buffers; \(\beta\) momentum coefficient; \(\beta_1,\beta_2\) Adam's decay rates; \(\rho\) RMSProp decay; \(\epsilon\) numerical floor; \(t\) step counter; \(\lambda\) weight-decay strength.

1 · Batch, stochastic and mini-batch descent

\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta-\eta\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}, \qquad \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_n \]

Worked with \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\tfrac12\theta^2\), \(\theta_0=4\), \(\eta=0.1\): \(\nabla\mathcal{L}=4\), so \(\theta_1=4-0.1(4)=3.6\) and \(\mathcal{L}_1=\tfrac12(3.6)^2=6.48\) (down from \(8.00\)).

Stochastic gradient descent replaces the full mean by one sample. With two samples whose gradients at \(\theta=4\) are \(3\) and \(1\), the batch update is \(4-0.1(2)=3.8\), while two SGD updates give \(4-0.1(3)=3.7\) then \(3.7-0.1(1)=3.6\). The SGD path jitters but reaches the same region with far less computation per step. A mini-batch of size 32 interpolates.

2 · Momentum

\[ v\leftarrow\beta v+\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L},\qquad \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta v \]

With \(\beta=0.9\), \(\eta=0.1\), \(\theta_0=4\), \(v_0=0\) and a constant gradient \(2\): \(v_1=2\), \(\theta_1=3.8\); \(v_2=0.9(2)+2=3.8\), \(\theta_2=3.8-0.38=3.42\); \(v_3=0.9(3.8)+2=5.42\), \(\theta_3=3.42-0.542=2.878\). The step grows from \(0.2\) to \(0.38\) to \(0.542\) because consistent gradients accumulate.

3 · RMSProp

\[ s\leftarrow\rho s+(1-\rho)(\nabla\mathcal{L})^2,\qquad \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\frac{\nabla\mathcal{L}}{\sqrt{s}+\epsilon} \]

With \(\rho=0.9\), \(\eta=0.1\), \(\theta_0=4\), \(s_0=0\), gradient \(2\) twice: \(s_1=0.9(0)+0.1(4)=0.4\), \(\sqrt{s_1}=0.6325\), step \(=0.1(2)/0.6325=0.3162\), \(\theta_1=3.6838\). Next \(s_2=0.9(0.4)+0.1(4)=0.76\), \(\sqrt{s_2}=0.8718\), step \(=0.2294\), \(\theta_2=3.4544\). The effective stride shrinks as the running squared gradient grows.

4 · Adam and bias correction

\[ m\leftarrow\beta_1 m+(1-\beta_1)\nabla,\quad s\leftarrow\beta_2 s+(1-\beta_2)\nabla^2,\quad \hat m=\frac{m}{1-\beta_1^t},\quad \hat s=\frac{s}{1-\beta_2^t},\quad \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s}+\epsilon} \]

With \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.999\), \(\eta=0.01\), \(\theta_0=4\), gradient \(2\) on both steps: \(m_1=0.2\), \(s_1=0.004\). Bias correction gives \(\hat m_1=0.2/(1-0.9)=2\) and \(\hat s_1=0.004/(1-0.999)=4\), so the step is \(0.01(2)/(2+10^{-8})\approx0.01\) and \(\theta_1=3.99\). Step 2: \(m_2=0.9(0.2)+0.2=0.38\Rightarrow\hat m_2=0.38/0.19=2\); \(s_2=0.999(0.004)+0.001(4)=0.007996\Rightarrow\hat s_2\approx4\), another \(0.01\) step, \(\theta_2=3.98\). Without correction the first step would be ten times too small.

5 · AdamW: decoupled decay

\[ \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\left(\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat s}+\epsilon}+\lambda\theta\right) \]

In Adam with L2 added to the loss, the \(\lambda\theta\) term is divided by the adaptive denominator, so the effective decay changes with the gradient history. AdamW applies decay directly. Worked with the Adam state above, \(\eta=0.01\), \(\lambda=0.1\): step 1 is \(0.01(2+0.1\cdot4)=0.024\), \(\theta_1=3.976\); step 2 is \(0.01(2+0.1\cdot3.976)=0.023976\), \(\theta_2=3.952024\).

6 · Learning rate meets curvature

For \(\mathcal{L}=\tfrac12k\theta^2\), gradient descent gives \(\theta_{t+1}=(1-\eta k)\theta_t\). Convergence needs \(|1-\eta k|<1\), hence \(0<\eta<2/k\).

Worked with \(k=100\): \(\eta=0.005\Rightarrow1-\eta k=0.5\), so \(\theta=1\) becomes \(0.5\) then \(0.25\). With \(\eta=0.02\Rightarrow1-\eta k=-1\), the parameter flips sign forever; with \(\eta=0.03\) it diverges. This is why a ravine (large \(k\) in one direction, small in another) is hard: a safe learning rate for the steep direction crawls along the flat one.

Lab: optimizer race

Five optimizers start from exactly the same point. Switch between a round quadratic, a ravine with 100× curvature in one direction, and a saddle with no minimum. The background colour is the loss evaluated on a grid; the trajectories and readouts are recomputed every step.

Code

One linear model, five optimizers, 200 steps each. The parameters are reset between runs so the comparison is fair; the printed final loss is produced at runtime. 

import torch

torch.manual_seed(0)
X = torch.linspace(-1, 1, 64).unsqueeze(1)        # shape (64, 1)
y = 2.0 * X + 1.0 + 0.05 * torch.randn_like(X)    # shape (64, 1)

w = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(0.0))
b = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(0.0))

def loss_fn():
    return ((w * X + b - y) ** 2).mean()          # MSE, mean reduction

configs = [
    ("SGD",           lambda: torch.optim.SGD([w, b], lr=0.1)),
    ("SGD+momentum",  lambda: torch.optim.SGD([w, b], lr=0.1, momentum=0.9)),
    ("RMSProp",       lambda: torch.optim.RMSprop([w, b], lr=0.05)),
    ("Adam",          lambda: torch.optim.Adam([w, b], lr=0.1)),
    ("AdamW",         lambda: torch.optim.AdamW([w, b], lr=0.1, weight_decay=0.05)),
]

for name, make_opt in configs:
    with torch.no_grad():
        w.fill_(0.0); b.fill_(0.0)                 # identical start for every run
    opt = make_opt()
    for step in range(200):
        opt.zero_grad()
        loss = loss_fn()
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
    print(f"{name:14s} final loss {loss_fn().item():.6f}  w={w.item():.3f} b={b.item():.3f}")

Common mistakes

Learning rate too high

A loss that grows and turns into nan usually means \(\eta\) exceeds roughly \(2/k\) for the sharpest curvature. Halve it until the first hundred steps are monotone.

Learning rate too low

A flat curve is not always convergence. Check the parameter norms: if they barely move, increase \(\eta\) by 10×. In a ravine, adapt the method rather than only the step size.

Weight decay is not always L2

With plain SGD, adding \(\lambda\theta\) to the gradient equals L2 on the loss. With adaptive methods it does not: AdamW's decoupled decay is the behaviour you usually want.

Momentum plus a large step

Velocity keeps growing while gradients agree, so a learning rate that is safe for SGD can diverge with \(\beta=0.9\). Reduce \(\eta\) when switching to momentum or Adam.

Debug this

An Adam run prints loss: nan at step 12. The model, data and loss are unchanged from a working SGD run. One line was edited: lr=10 in the Adam constructor.

Show the likely cause and the fix

The learning rate is 10, roughly four orders of magnitude too large for Adam's default step scale. The first update catapults the parameters into a region where the loss overflows. Set lr=1e-3 (or at most 1e-2 on a tiny toy problem) and the run recovers.

Second check: print the gradient norm on step 1. If it is finite but the parameters become inf, the update size is the culprit; if the gradient itself is nan, inspect the loss (log of zero, division by zero, or exploded activations).

Summary

1 · All optimizers change only the update rule: θ ← θ − η·(some transform of the gradient).
2 · Momentum accumulates velocity, RMSProp rescales by recent squared gradients, Adam combines both with bias correction, and AdamW decays θ directly.
3 · Stability is governed by curvature: 0 < η < 2/k for a quadratic; use a larger step in flat directions and a smaller one in ravines.

Cheat sheet — M0·03

  • SGD: \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}\).
  • Momentum: \(v\leftarrow\beta v+\nabla\), \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta v\).
  • RMSProp: \(s\leftarrow\rho s+(1-\rho)g^2\), \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta g/(\sqrt{s}+\epsilon)\).
  • Adam: \(\hat m=m/(1-\beta_1^t)\), \(\hat s=s/(1-\beta_2^t)\), \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\hat m/(\sqrt{\hat s}+\epsilon)\).
  • AdamW: add \(-\eta\lambda\theta\) directly; no division by \(\sqrt{\hat s}\).
  • Stable step for \(\tfrac12k\theta^2\): \(0<\eta<2/k\).
  • Typical defaults: \(\eta=10^{-3}\) for Adam, \(\eta=0.1\) for SGD; \(\beta_1=0.9,\beta_2=0.999,\rho=0.9\).
