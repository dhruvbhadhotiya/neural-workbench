Initialization and debugging training

— · Lesson 4 of 5 · Exact math labs

Most training failures are decided before the first step. Get the weight scale right, then read two curves to tell underfitting from a bug.

Objectives

  • Compute Xavier and He scales from fan-in and fan-out.
  • Predict vanishing and exploding activations from a variance calculation.
  • Diagnose underfitting, overfitting and data bugs from train/validation curves.
  • Run the overfit-one-batch recipe as a capacity sanity check.

You know this from ML → here it becomes

Bias–variance in classical ML is diagnosed with one validation curve. In deep learning you watch two curves — training and validation — plus the scale of activations. A model that cannot overfit a single batch is broken, not merely under-regularized.

Intuition: keep the signal alive

Each layer multiplies its input by a matrix. If the weights are too small, the signal shrinks by a constant factor at every layer and the last layer sees almost zero; the gradients shrink the same way on the return trip. If the weights are too large, activations explode or saturate and gradients vanish for a different reason. Good initialization keeps the variance of activations roughly constant from input to output.

The rule depends on the nonlinearity. Sigmoid and tanh are roughly linear near zero, so they want a variance that balances fan-in and fan-out (Xavier). ReLU zeroes half its inputs, so it needs twice the variance (He). Everything else is a debugging exercise: first verify the model can memorize a tiny dataset, then worry about generalization.

Init sets the scale; the learning curve tells the story. If train loss will not go to nearly zero on one batch, fix the pipeline before adding data or regularization.

The math

Symbols

\(n_{in}\), \(n_{out}\) fan-in and fan-out of a layer; \(w\) a weight; \(z_l\) pre-activation at layer \(l\); \(a_l=\phi(z_l)\) its activation; \(N\) batch size; \(\eta\) learning rate; \(\mathcal{L}\) loss; \(\lambda\) regularization strength; \(\varepsilon\) a small constant. Assume independent weights and inputs with zero mean.

1 · Variance propagation

\[ \mathrm{Var}(z_l)=n_{in}\,\mathrm{Var}(w)\,\mathrm{Var}(a_{l-1}) \]

Worked with \(n_{in}=256\), \(\mathrm{Var}(w)=0.01\) and standardized inputs with \(\mathrm{Var}(a)=1\): \(\mathrm{Var}(z)=256(0.01)(1)=2.56\), so the typical pre-activation is \(\sqrt{2.56}=1.6\). Repeat with \(\mathrm{Var}(w)=10^{-4}\): \(\mathrm{Var}(z)=0.0256\) and std \(0.16\); after ten such layers the variance is multiplied by \(0.0256^{10}\approx1.2\times10^{-16}\) — the signal is gone.

2 · Xavier (Glorot) scale

\[ w\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\frac{2}{n_{in}+n_{out}}\right) \]

Worked for a \(784\to256\) layer: variance \(2/(784+256)=2/1040=0.001923\), std \(=\sqrt{0.001923}=0.04385\). The forward variance is \(784(0.001923)=1.5077\), std \(1.228\); the backward gain is \(256(0.001923)=0.4923\). Xavier is the compromise between the two directions, which is why it pairs naturally with sigmoid and tanh. Uniform Xavier uses \(U[-\sqrt{6/(n_{in}+n_{out})},\,\sqrt{6/(n_{in}+n_{out})}]\); for \(784\to256\) the bound is \(\sqrt{6/1040}=0.07596\).

3 · He scale

\[ w\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\frac{2}{n_{in}}\right) \]

Same \(784\to256\) layer: variance \(2/784=0.002551\), std \(0.05051\). Forward \(\mathrm{Var}(z)=784(0.002551)=2.00\), std \(1.414\). ReLU keeps only positive values, so for symmetric \(z\), \(\mathrm{Var}(a)\approx\tfrac12\mathrm{Var}(z)=1.00\) — exactly the scale we want to pass to the next layer.

4 · Why the naive scales fail

With \(w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.01^2)\), \(\mathrm{Var}(w)=10^{-4}\). For \(n_{in}=784\): \(\mathrm{Var}(z)=0.0784\), std \(0.28\); after ten layers the variance factor is \(0.0784^{10}\approx8.8\times10^{-12}\). With \(w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10^2)\), \(\mathrm{Var}(w)=100\), so \(\mathrm{Var}(z)=78400\) and std \(280\): tanh/sigmoid saturate to \(\pm1\) or \(0/1\), where their derivatives are essentially zero.

A related failure is the dead ReLU. If \(z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\) with a large negative bias \(\mu=-3,\sigma=1\), the alive fraction is \(P(z>0)=1-\Phi(3)\approx0.00135\), about 0.14%. Units that never activate receive zero gradient forever under ReLU.

5 · Reading learning curves

\[ \text{gap}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}-\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}} \]

Worked with \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{train}}=0.02\), \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{val}}=0.85\): gap \(=0.83\), a clear overfitting signature. With both losses around \(0.9\) on a 10-class task the model is underfitting (chance is roughly \(2.30\) nats of cross-entropy, but the shape matters more than the absolute value). A falling train loss with a rising validation loss means stop early or regularize; two flat curves mean check the pipeline.

The overfit-one-batch test isolates the second case: take 32 examples, train until training loss is near zero. If it will not fall, the fault is in the model, loss, optimizer or data plumbing — not in the amount of data. If it does fall, capacity is fine and you can work on generalization.

Code

A complete PyTorch training loop with train/validation curves and the one-batch sanity check. The synthetic target is nonlinear on purpose, so a too-shallow model visibly underfits. 

import torch
from torch import nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
N = 600
X = torch.randn(N, 2)
y = ((X[:, 0] * X[:, 1] > 0).long())                # a nonlinear 2-D rule
perm = torch.randperm(N)
X, y = X[perm], y[perm]                              # shuffle BEFORE splitting
Xtr, ytr = X[:480], y[:480]
Xva, yva = X[480:], y[480:]

def make_model():
    return nn.Sequential(
        nn.Linear(2, 32), nn.ReLU(),
        nn.Linear(32, 32), nn.ReLU(),
        nn.Linear(32, 2),                            # logits, shape (N, 2)
    )

model = make_model()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-2)  # He init is nn.Linear's default
loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()                      # logits in, index labels

for epoch in range(1, 201):
    model.train()                                    # dropout / batchnorm behave in train mode
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss = loss_fn(model(Xtr), ytr)
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
    if epoch % 40 == 0:
        model.eval()                                 # freeze BN statistics, disable dropout
        with torch.no_grad():                        # no graph needed for evaluation
            tr = loss_fn(model(Xtr), ytr).item()
            va = loss_fn(model(Xva), yva).item()
            acc = (model(Xva).argmax(dim=1) == yva).float().mean().item()
        print(f"epoch {epoch:3d}  train {tr:.4f}  val {va:.4f}  val acc {acc:.3f}")

# Capacity check: if this does not go to ~0, debug the pipeline before tuning anything.
probe = make_model()
probe_opt = torch.optim.Adam(probe.parameters(), lr=1e-2)
for step in range(200):
    probe_opt.zero_grad()
    l = loss_fn(probe(Xtr[:32]), ytr[:32])
    l.backward()
    probe_opt.step()
print("one-batch loss", round(l.item(), 6))          # expect close to zero

Common mistakes

randn * 0.01

A classic but badly scaled default. With hundreds of fan-in weights the pre-activations shrink toward zero, gradients vanish and the loss barely moves. Use Xavier or He.

randn * 10

The opposite failure: saturated sigmoids, huge ReLU outputs and exploding gradients. If the first batch produces a loss in the thousands, inspect the init scale first.

Missing model.train()

Dropout and batch normalization behave differently in train and eval mode. Forgetting model.eval() at validation makes metrics jump; forgetting model.train() afterwards quietly disables regularization.

Leakage through normalization

Computing mean and variance on the full dataset before splitting leaks validation statistics into training. Fit scalers on the training split only, then apply them to validation.

Debug this

A 10-class classifier reports about 10% validation accuracy even though its training loss falls. The model has enough capacity and the optimizer is Adam with a sensible learning rate. The data loader does this: X_train = X[:900], y_train = y[:900], but the labels were sorted before that line.

Show the likely cause and the fix

The labels were not shuffled together with the inputs. The first 900 rows may all belong to one or two classes, so the network learns a constant prediction and validation accuracy sits at chance. Apply the same random permutation to both arrays before splitting, or use a DataLoader with shuffle=True over a TensorDataset.

Quick check: print torch.bincount(y_train). If it is concentrated in one or two bins while the full label histogram is balanced, the split — not the model — is the bug.

Summary

1 · Keep variance stable: Var(z) = n_in Var(w) Var(a), giving Xavier 2/(n_in+n_out) for tanh/sigmoid and He 2/n_in for ReLU.
2 · Too-small weights vanish the signal; too-large weights saturate or explode; zeros break symmetry completely.
3 · Verify capacity with one batch, then read train and validation curves: a large gap means overfitting, two flat curves mean a pipeline bug.

Cheat sheet — M0·04

  • \(\mathrm{Var}(z_l)=n_{in}\mathrm{Var}(w)\mathrm{Var}(a_{l-1})\).
  • Xavier normal: \(\sigma=\sqrt{2/(n_{in}+n_{out})}\) — 0.0439 for 784→256.
  • He normal: \(\sigma=\sqrt{2/n_{in}}\) — 0.0505 for 784→256.
  • Naive \(\sigma=0.01\): Var(z)=0.0784 at fan-in 784 — vanishing.
  • Naive \(\sigma=10\): Var(z)=78,400 — saturation and explosion.
  • Overfit one batch first; then compare \(\mathcal{L}_{val}-\mathcal{L}_{train}\).
  • Always shuffle inputs and labels together before splitting; fit scalers on train only.
