Variational autoencoders: learn a distribution

— · Lesson 2 of 5 · Exact + Illustrative labs

A plain autoencoder gives you points in latent space. A VAE gives you a distribution you can sample from — and the ELBO tells you exactly what that freedom costs.

Objectives

  • Derive the ELBO in two lines from the KL between an approximate posterior and the true one.
  • Compute the Gaussian KL divergence in closed form, including the \(\mu=1,\sigma=1\) case.
  • Implement the reparameterisation trick and explain where the gradient flows.
  • Predict what a larger \(\beta\) does to the latent distribution and to reconstruction.

You know this from ML → here it becomes

In Lesson 1 the encoder returned one vector \(\mathbf h\) per input. Here it returns the parameters of a distribution: a mean \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and a standard deviation \(\boldsymbol\sigma\). The decoder sees a sample \(\mathbf z\) instead of a fixed code. Adding noise to the bottleneck would normally block backpropagation; the reparameterisation trick moves the randomness to a parameter-free input \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\), and the gradients flow through \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\sigma\) as usual.

Intuition: a cloud, not a point

Let \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^D\) be an input, \(\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^{D_z}\) a latent vector, and \(\theta,\phi\) the decoder and encoder parameters. The encoder defines \(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu(\mathbf x),\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\sigma^2(\mathbf x)))\); the decoder defines \(p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)\). The prior is the fixed standard normal \(p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,I)\). Symbols: \(D\) is the input width, \(D_z\) the latent width, \(I\) the identity matrix, and \(\mathcal N\) a Gaussian density.

Maximising only reconstruction pushes every posterior to a narrow spike — the ordinary autoencoder we already met. The KL term pulls each posterior toward the prior. Together they make the aggregate posterior over the dataset cover the prior, so a random \(\mathbf z\) lands somewhere the decoder has seen. That is why a VAE can generate.

ELBO = expected reconstruction log-likelihood minus KL to the prior. Low reconstruction means faithful output; low KL means the latent codes are organised like the prior. \(\beta\) is the price tag on the second term.

The math

1 · The ELBO in two lines

We want to maximise \(\log p_\theta(\mathbf x)\), but it needs the intractable posterior \(p_\theta(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\). Introduce \(q_\phi\) and use Jensen's inequality on \(\log \mathbb E_{q}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)}{q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)}\right]\):

\[ \log p_\theta(\mathbf x)\;\ge\;\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)\right]}_{\text{expected reconstruction log-likelihood}}\;-\;\underbrace{D_{KL}\!\left(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\,\|\,p(\mathbf z)\right)}_{\text{complexity cost}} \;=\; \mathcal L_{\text{ELBO}} \]

Equivalently, \(\log p_\theta(\mathbf x)=\mathcal L_{\text{ELBO}}+D_{KL}(q_\phi\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x))\): the gap is itself a KL, so maximising the ELBO minimises the true posterior approximation error. We minimise the loss \(-\mathcal L_{\text{ELBO}}\) in code.

Worked example. Take one synthetic input with expected reconstruction log-likelihood \(\mathbb E[\log p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)]=-1.20\) nats and \(D_{KL}(q\|p)=0.25\) nats. Then \(\mathcal L_{\text{ELBO}}=-1.20-0.25=-1.45\) nats and the training loss is \(+1.45\) nats. Lower loss means a better bound — ELBO values are negative, so “larger is better” only applies to the bound itself.

2 · Closed-form Gaussian KL

With \(q=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol\sigma^2))\) and \(p=\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,I)\), the KL has a closed form (no sampling needed). For one latent coordinate:

\[ D_{KL}\!\left(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(0,1)\right) = \tfrac12\left(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2\right) = -\tfrac12\left(1+\log\sigma^2-\mu^2-\sigma^2\right) \]

For \(D_z\) independent coordinates, sum this over \(j=1,\ldots,D_z\). It is zero exactly when \(\mu=0,\sigma=1\) in every coordinate, i.e. \(q=p\).

Worked example (requested case). Let \(\mu=1\), \(\sigma=1\). Then \(\sigma^2=1\) and \(\log\sigma^2=\log 1=0\), so \(D_{KL}=\tfrac12(1^2+1^2-1-0)=\tfrac12(1)=0.5\) nats. The mean being one standard deviation away from the prior mean costs half a nat. Check the other direction: \(\mu=0,\sigma=0.5\) gives \(\tfrac12(0+0.25-1-\log 0.25)=\tfrac12(-0.75+1.3863)=0.3182\) nats.

3 · Reparameterisation

Sampling \(\mathbf z\sim q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\) directly makes the sampling step non-differentiable with respect to \(\phi\). Write instead

\[ \mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon,\qquad \boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,I) \]

where \(\odot\) is elementwise multiplication. Now \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\) is a parameter-free random input, so \(\partial z_j/\partial\mu_j=1\) and \(\partial z_j/\partial\sigma_j=\epsilon_j\) are ordinary derivatives. This is the trick that makes a VAE trainable by backpropagation.

Worked example. With \(\mu=1,\sigma=2\) and a seeded draw \(\epsilon=-0.5\), \(z=1+2(-0.5)=0\). The gradients through this sample are \(\partial z/\partial\mu=1\) and \(\partial z/\partial\sigma=\epsilon=-0.5\): increasing \(\sigma\) by \(0.1\) would move this particular sample down by \(0.05\). Averaged over many seeded \(\epsilon\), the expected gradient is still correct.

4 · \(\beta\)-VAE and conditioning

A \(\beta\)-VAE puts a weight \(\beta\) on the KL term: \(\mathcal L_\beta=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)]-\beta D_{KL}(q\|p)\). \(\beta=1\) is the exact ELBO; \(\beta>1\) buys a more prior-like, often more disentangled latent code at the price of blurrier reconstructions. A conditional VAE (cVAE) simply passes a condition \(c\) into both networks: \(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x,c)\) and \(p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z,c)\).

Worked example. Use the \(-1.20\) nats reconstruction and \(0.25\) nats KL from above. With \(\beta=4\), \(\mathcal L_4=-1.20-4(0.25)=-2.20\), so the training loss is \(2.20\) instead of \(1.45\). For conditioning, a 2-D input \(\mathbf x\) plus a 3-class one-hot \(c\) gives a \(2+3=5\)-dimensional encoder input: \((N,5)\) in a batch of \(N\) rows.

Code

A VAE on synthetic 2-D data: the encoder emits \(\mu\) and \(\log\sigma^2\), the reparameterisation produces \((N,D_z)\), and the loss prints its two terms separately. Shapes are written as \((N,D)\) because this data is a point cloud, not an image batch. Replace the two lines marked dataset swap with MNIST to see the same code on \((N,1,28,28)\). 

import torch
from torch import nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
D, DZ = 2, 2
x = torch.randn(256, D)                      # synthetic data, shape (N=256, D=2)

class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(D, 32), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(32, 2 * DZ))   # (N, 2) -> (N, 4)
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(DZ, 32), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(32, D))         # (N, 2) -> (N, 2)

    def forward(self, x):
        stats = self.enc(x)                  # (N, 4): [mu | logvar]
        mu, logvar = stats.chunk(2, dim=1)    # each (N, DZ=2)
        eps = torch.randn_like(mu)            # (N, DZ), parameter-free noise
        z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * eps   # reparameterisation, (N, DZ)
        return self.dec(z), mu, logvar        # reconstruction, (N, 2)

model = VAE()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

for step in range(1500):
    xhat, mu, logvar = model(x)              # xhat (N, 2); mu, logvar (N, 2)
    rec = F.mse_loss(xhat, x, reduction="mean")   # mean over N and D
    kl = -0.5 * (1 + logvar - mu.pow(2) - logvar.exp()).sum(dim=1).mean()
    loss = rec + kl                          # beta = 1: negative ELBO
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

with torch.no_grad():
    z = torch.randn(16, DZ)                  # prior samples, shape (16, 2)
    samples = model.dec(z)                   # generated data, shape (16, 2)
print("rec", round(rec.item(), 4), "kl", round(kl.item(), 4))
import tensorflow as tf
from tensorflow import keras
from tensorflow.keras import layers

tf.random.set_seed(0)
D, DZ = 2, 2
x = tf.random.normal([256, D])                # synthetic data, shape (N=256, D=2)

enc_in = keras.Input(shape=(D,))             # (None, 2)
h = layers.Dense(32, activation="relu")(enc_in)
mu = layers.Dense(DZ, name="mu")(h)          # (None, DZ=2)
logvar = layers.Dense(DZ, name="logvar")(h)  # (None, DZ=2)

class Sample(keras.layers.Layer):
    def call(self, args):                    # z = mu + sigma * eps
        mu, logvar = args
        eps = tf.random.normal(tf.shape(mu)) # same shape as mu
        return mu + tf.exp(0.5 * logvar) * eps

z = Sample()([mu, logvar])                   # (None, DZ)
dec = layers.Dense(32, activation="relu")(z)
out = layers.Dense(D)(dec)                   # (None, 2)
vae = keras.Model(enc_in, out)

def vae_loss(y_true, y_pred):
    rec = tf.reduce_mean(tf.square(y_pred - y_true))  # mean over N and D
    kl = -0.5 * tf.reduce_mean(tf.reduce_sum(
        1 + logvar - tf.square(mu) - tf.exp(logvar), axis=1))
    return rec + kl

vae.compile(optimizer=keras.optimizers.Adam(1e-3), loss=vae_loss)
vae.fit(x, x, epochs=30, batch_size=32, verbose=0)
print("mu shape", mu.shape, "z shape", z.shape)  # (None, 2), (None, 2)

The trainer logs the two terms separately on purpose: if KL falls to zero while reconstruction is poor, the model has collapsed. Clamping logvar (for example torch.clamp(logvar, -10, 10)) prevents exp from overflowing on a bad batch. The Keras listing is the same arithmetic with a custom sampling layer; note that Keras works in NHWC internally for images, though this toy data is 2-D.

Common mistakes

KL weight too high

With very large \(\beta\), the KL term dominates and the encoder outputs the prior for every input — posterior collapse. Reconstructions become generic and the latent code carries no information about \(\mathbf x\). Raise \(\beta\) gradually and watch both terms.

exp on unclamped logvar

The decoder uses \(\sigma=\exp(\log\sigma^2/2)\). A single large logvar from an unstable batch can overflow to inf and poison training. Clamp logvar to a safe range before exponentiating.

Sampling without no_grad

Drawing prior samples for inspection is inference, not training. Wrap it in with torch.no_grad(): to avoid keeping an autograd graph for samples you will only display. The reparameterised training sample, by contrast, must keep its graph.

Comparing ELBO across datasets

The ELBO is a log-density in nats for a specific data representation: changing from normalised pixel values to 0–255 integers changes reconstruction scale and bitwise discretisation, so the numbers are not comparable. Compare models on the same data and the same likelihood definition.

Debug this

A reader reports: “KL drops to 0 and every generated sample looks the same.” Training ran happily. The loss implementation is the culprit: 

def vae_loss(x, xhat, mu, logvar):
    rec = F.mse_loss(xhat, x)
    kl = -0.5 * (1 + logvar - mu.pow(2) - logvar.exp()).sum(dim=1).mean()
    return 1000.0 * rec + kl    # BUG: reconstruction is effectively ignored
Show the likely cause and the fix

The reconstruction is multiplied by 1000 while the KL is left at weight 1, so the cheapest descent is to drive KL to zero: the encoder outputs \(\mu=0,\log\sigma^2=0\) for every input, and the decoder learns a single average output. This is posterior collapse induced by a scaling mistake. Use matched scales (start with weight 1 on both terms), standardise the inputs, and check that reconstructions still vary with \(\mathbf x\) before tuning \(\beta\).

Quick detector: print the standard deviation of \(\boldsymbol\mu\) across a batch. If it is near zero while KL is near zero, the encoder has stopped listening to the input.

Summary

1 · The ELBO is \(\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)]-D_{KL}(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\|p(\mathbf z))\); with \(\mu=1,\sigma=1\), the Gaussian KL is \(\tfrac12(1+1-1-0)=0.5\) nats.
2 · Reparameterisation \(\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon\) makes the random draw parameter-free, so \(\partial z/\partial\mu=1\) and \(\partial z/\partial\sigma=\epsilon\).
3 · Larger \(\beta\) prices the KL more heavily: the latent cloud looks more like the prior while reconstructions blur, and \(\beta=1\) is the exact ELBO.

Cheat sheet — M4·02

  • \(\mathcal L_{\text{ELBO}}=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x|z)]-D_{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)\|p(z))\). Worked: −1.20 − 0.25 = −1.45 nats.
  • \(D_{KL}(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\|\mathcal N(0,1))=\tfrac12(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\log\sigma^2)\). Worked: μ=1, σ=1 → 0.5.
  • Reparameterisation: \(z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon\), \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\); derivatives 1 and ε.
  • Train on \(-\mathcal L_{\text{ELBO}}\); implement with \(\mu\) and \(\log\sigma^2\) outputs, and clamp logvar before exp.
  • \(\beta\)-VAE: \(-\mathbb E_q[\log p]+\beta D_{KL}\); β>1 → more prior-like latents, blurrier output.
  • cVAE: \(q_\phi(z|x,c)\), \(p_\theta(x|z,c)\). Detector: μ variance ≈ 0 means posterior collapse.
